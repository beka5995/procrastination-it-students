# Research Design

## Topic

**Procrastination Triggers in the Digital Environment: How Context Switching and Micro-Habits Affect the Concentration of IT Students**

## General Aim

To identify the most common disruptive digital habits among IT students and examine how context switching and multitasking are related to cognitive load, reduced concentration, and academic procrastination.

## RQ1

**Question:** Which disruptive digital habits are most common among IT students during academic and practical tasks?

**H0:** There are no statistically significant differences in the frequency of the main types of digital distraction.

**H1:** Some types of digital distraction occur significantly more often than others.

### Variables
- Factor / category compared: distraction type
- Outcomes: distraction frequency and perceived habit strength
- Recorded controls: task complexity, study format, device setup, academic motivation

## RQ2

**Question:** How do frequent context switching and multitasking affect perceived cognitive load and mental fatigue during IT-related academic tasks?

**H0:** Context switching and multitasking have no statistically significant relationship with perceived cognitive load and mental fatigue.

**H1:** Higher context-switching frequency and multitasking are associated with higher cognitive load and mental fatigue. In the controlled task, the interrupted condition is expected to produce higher cognitive load than the focused condition.

### Variables
- Predictors: context-switching frequency, background multitasking
- Experimental factor: focused vs interrupted condition
- Outcomes: CLI, mental fatigue, task accuracy, completion time
- Controls: task difficulty, session duration, programming experience, sleep

## RQ3

**Question:** How is cognitive overload caused by digital distractions related to academic procrastination?

**H0:** Cognitive load has no statistically significant relationship with the frequency or intensity of academic procrastination.

**H1:** Higher cognitive load is associated with more frequent and more intense academic procrastination.

### Variables
- Predictor: CLI
- Outcomes: PI and unrelated-task switching
- Controls: motivation, task difficulty/importance, deadline proximity, sleep, workload

## Controlled Task

Each volunteer completes two 20-minute conditions:

1. **Focused baseline:** notifications disabled; no unrelated applications.
2. **Interrupted condition:** standardized interruption prompts requiring brief attention shifts.

The order of conditions should be counterbalanced. Two tasks of comparable difficulty are used so that Task A and Task B are not always tied to the same condition.

## Main Statistics

- RQ1: descriptive statistics + Friedman test
- RQ2 survey: Spearman correlation; optional multiple regression
- RQ2 controlled task: paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test
- RQ3: Spearman correlation + multiple regression
- alpha = 0.05
- effect sizes are reported where relevant
- Cronbach's alpha is used to check the internal consistency of CLI and PI

## Interpretation Rule

Survey associations are not treated as proof of causation. Stronger causal interpretation is limited to the short-term focused-vs-interrupted controlled comparison.
